This data provides unprecedented opportunities to understand the factors that influence race outcomes, optimize team strategies, and predict competitive dynamics. The intersection of data science and motorsport has transformed how teams approach race preparation, real-time decision-making, and long-term development, making Formula 1 one of the most data-intensive sports in the world. Each Grand Prix weekend produces millions of data points from practice sessions, qualifying rounds, and race day, creating a rich analytical landscape for exploring performance patterns, technological advantages, and competitive strategies.

The significance of data analysis in Formula 1 extends beyond team operations to influence regulatory decisions, broadcast coverage, and fan engagement. Teams invest heavily in data infrastructure, employing hundreds of engineers and analysts who process telemetry data in real-time during races and conduct detailed post-race analysis to gain competitive advantages. Race strategy decisions regarding tire compounds, pit stop timing, fuel loads, and overtaking opportunities increasingly rely on sophisticated predictive models that synthesize historical performance data, real-time conditions, and competitor behavior. The sport's technical regulations evolve annually, creating new challenges and opportunities for teams to exploit data-driven insights in car development and race execution. Furthermore, the global nature of Formula 1, with races held across diverse circuits ranging from high-speed tracks like Monza to technical street circuits like Monaco, produces varied performance characteristics that demand adaptive analytical approaches. Understanding these patterns through comprehensive data analysis reveals how track characteristics, weather conditions, tire management, and technical innovations interact to determine competitive outcomes.

Current research in Formula 1 data science has explored various dimensions of race performance, including lap time prediction, optimal pit stop strategies, tire degradation modeling, and driver performance evaluation. Academic studies have applied machine learning techniques to predict race winners, classify driver skill levels, and optimize team strategies based on historical data patterns. Industry practitioners have developed sophisticated simulation tools that model race scenarios, enabling teams to test strategic options before making critical decisions during live races. The availability of public timing data through official channels and community-developed APIs like FastF1 has democratized access to Formula 1 telemetry, enabling researchers and enthusiasts to conduct independent analyses and develop innovative analytical methodologies. Despite these advances, significant opportunities remain to deepen understanding of performance factors, particularly in areas such as tire compound selection under varying conditions, the impact of aerodynamic regulations on competitive balance, and the quantification of driver contributions relative to car performance.

This data science project investigates Formula 1 racing performance through comprehensive analysis of the 2024 season, focusing on lap times, tire strategies, driver performance, and race dynamics across multiple Grand Prix events. The analysis examines how various factors including tire compound selection, tire degradation over race distance, track characteristics, and race phase influence lap time performance and competitive outcomes. By gathering and analyzing official timing data, this research seeks to uncover patterns in racing performance that enhance understanding of competitive dynamics. The project utilizes data gathered through the FastF1 API, which provides access to official Formula 1 timing data including lap times, sector times, speed measurements, tire information, and race positions, creating a robust foundation for multi-dimensional analysis.

Building on the exploratory analysis established in Module 1, this project now applies three advanced machine learning techniques to extract deeper insights from the data. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is used to reduce dimensionality and identify the most important performance variables. Clustering methods — KMeans, Hierarchical, and DBSCAN — reveal natural groupings within lap data that illuminate strategic and performance patterns. Association Rule Mining (ARM) uncovers co-occurring strategic events across laps, revealing the hidden logic of tire strategy and race phase decisions. Together, these methods transform raw telemetry into actionable intelligence about what truly drives competitive outcomes in Formula 1.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

What is PCA?

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is an unsupervised dimensionality reduction technique that transforms high-dimensional data into a lower-dimensional space while retaining as much variance (information) as possible. PCA works by identifying the directions — called principal components — along which the data varies the most. These directions are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix of the standardized data, and the corresponding eigenvalues indicate how much variance each component captures. The first principal component captures the greatest variance, the second captures the next greatest (orthogonal to the first), and so on. By projecting data onto the top k principal components, we reduce dimensionality while preserving the majority of the information, enabling visualization, noise reduction, and more efficient downstream modeling.

Normalization using StandardScaler

The data was normalized using sklearn.preprocessing.StandardScaler, which transforms each feature to have a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. This step is essential because PCA is sensitive to scale — without normalization, features with larger numerical ranges (e.g. speeds in km/h) would dominate the principal components regardless of their actual importance.

Clustering

(a) Compare and Contrast: KMeans, Hierarchical, and DBSCAN

KMeans (Partition Clustering) partitions data into k groups by minimizing within-cluster variance. It is fast, scalable, and produces interpretable centroid-based clusters, but requires specifying k in advance and assumes convex, similarly-sized clusters. It assigns every point to a cluster — outliers are forced into the nearest group.

Hierarchical Clustering builds a tree (dendrogram) of merges by progressively combining the most similar points or clusters. No k needs to be specified upfront — you cut the dendrogram at any height to get any number of clusters. Ward linkage (used here) minimizes total within-cluster variance at each merge. The tradeoff is computational cost: O(n²) makes it impractical for very large datasets. The dendrogram visualizes the full hierarchy of relationships, revealing how similar clusters are to each other.

DBSCAN (Density-Based Clustering) groups points that are densely packed together, labeling sparse regions as noise (−1). It automatically discovers the number of clusters, handles clusters of arbitrary shape, and explicitly identifies outlier laps — a key advantage for racing data that contains anomalous laps like formation laps and safety car periods. The key parameters are eps (neighborhood radius) and min_samples (minimum points to form a dense region).

Summary comparison:

KMeans: requires k, no noise handling, spherical clusters, very fast

Hierarchical: no k required, no noise handling, any shape, slow O(n²)

DBSCAN: no k required, handles noise/outliers, any shape, fast O(n log n)

Association Rule Mining (ARM)

(a) Overview of ARM

Association Rule Mining (ARM) is an unsupervised machine learning technique for discovering interesting relationships — called association rules — between variables in large transaction datasets. It was originally developed for market-basket analysis but applies to any domain where co-occurrences are meaningful. In Formula 1, we treat each lap as a transaction and discover which strategic events tend to occur together.

A rule takes the form A → B: "if A appears in a transaction, then B also tends to appear." Three measures evaluate rule quality:

Support: How often an itemset appears in the dataset. support(A) = count(A) / total_transactions. High support means the pattern is common.

Confidence: How often the rule is correct. confidence(A→B) = support(A ∪ B) / support(A). High confidence means when A occurs, B reliably follows.

Lift: How much more likely B is given A, compared to B's base rate. lift(A→B) = confidence(A→B) / support(B). Lift > 1 means A and B co-occur more than by chance. Lift is typically the most useful measure for finding truly interesting rules.

The Apriori Algorithm is the classic method for finding frequent itemsets efficiently. It relies on the anti-monotone principle: if an itemset is infrequent, all its supersets must also be infrequent. Starting from single items, Apriori iteratively generates candidate itemsets of increasing size, pruning any candidate that contains an infrequent subset. This dramatically reduces the search space compared to brute force enumeration.

(b) Data Preparation for ARM

ARM requires unlabeled transaction data — each row represents a basket of items that co-occurred, with Boolean values (True/False) indicating item presence. There are no numeric values and no target labels. In our F1 context, each lap is one transaction and items are categorical events describing what occurred on that lap.

To create this format, continuous variables were discretized into categories: lap times were binned into FAST / MEDIUM / SLOW thirds per race; race progress was divided into EARLY / MID / LATE phases; pit stop occurrence was flagged as YES / NO. Tire compound (SOFT / MEDIUM / HARD) and Team were kept as-is since they are already categorical.

(e) Conclusions

SOFT → FAST lap is the strongest rule (high confidence and lift ~2.3), confirming that soft tires reliably produce faster laps. The high lift value quantifies how much more likely a FAST lap is on soft tires compared to the overall average.

HARD → LATE race phase is a frequently occurring association, reflecting the classic F1 one-stop strategy of starting on soft or medium tires and finishing on hard compounds. Teams actively engineer this pattern to maximize overall race performance.

PitStop + MEDIUM → MID race phase reveals that medium tire pit stops are overwhelmingly a mid-race phenomenon. Teams using medium compounds tend to pit earlier than those on hard tires, bridging between an initial soft stint and a final harder compound.

These findings provide actionable strategic intelligence: knowing these associations allows a team to anticipate competitor pit timing, predict likely tire choices, and plan their own strategy to gain competitive advantage. ARM transforms raw lap data into a readable map of the hidden strategic logic governing modern Formula 1 races.

Conclusions

This project applied three advanced machine learning techniques — PCA, clustering, and association rule mining — to Formula 1 lap data from the 2024 season to extract insights about racing performance, tire strategy, and competitive dynamics.

PCA Findings

Tire compound and overall lap pace are the dominant sources of variance in F1 performance data, captured by the first two principal components alone (77.4%). Five components are sufficient to retain 95% of the data's information, enabling dramatic dimensionality reduction with minimal information loss. The feature loading analysis revealed that PC1 represents overall pace (lap time + sector times), PC2 represents straight-line speed (SpeedI1, SpeedFL), and PC3 captures tire aging effects (TyreLife).

Clustering Findings

All three clustering methods — KMeans, hierarchical, and DBSCAN — converge on tire compound as the primary grouping structure, with 3 clusters mapping cleanly to SOFT / MEDIUM / HARD. Secondary clusters at finer granularity reveal track-specific performance bands. DBSCAN's identification of ~1.7% outlier laps highlights that anomalous laps (formation laps, safety car periods, pit outlaps) must be handled carefully in any predictive pipeline.

ARM Findings

The Apriori algorithm uncovered clear strategic patterns: SOFT tires reliably produce FAST laps (lift ~2.3), HARD tires cluster in LATE race phases, and MEDIUM tire pit stops predominantly occur mid-race. These patterns quantitatively confirm the strategic logic that expert commentators observe qualitatively, and could directly inform real-time strategy tools used by teams during races.

Answers to Research Questions

Q1 — Tire compound vs. lap time: SOFT tires produce the fastest laps across all circuit types, with HARD tires typically 1.5–3 seconds slower per lap depending on the circuit.

Q2 — Tire age vs. degradation: Lap time degrades approximately linearly with tire age at ~0.08–0.12 seconds per lap, with SOFT tires showing steeper degradation than HARD.

Q5 — Tire degradation predicting pit timing: ARM rules confirm that teams can be characterized by their tire strategy archetypes, with clear associations between compound choice and race phase that effectively predict when pit stops will occur.

Q6 — Correlates of competitive lap times: The correlation heatmap and PCA loadings show that Sector 1 time is most strongly correlated with overall lap time, followed by Sector 3 — indicating that the first sector is the primary differentiator of lap time performance.
